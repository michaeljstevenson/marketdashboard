Commentary

As of the October 7, 2026 close

Over five years, the S&P 500's return has been almost entirely an earnings story. Its 14.3% a year breaks down into 14.6% from EPS growth, 1.3% from dividends and -1.6% from a lower multiple, as the P/E slipped from 25.9 to 24.0.

The shorter windows look different. Over three years the multiple rose from 20.7 to 24.0, adding 5.1% a year on top of 16.9% earnings growth. Over the last year earnings grew 25.4% but the P/E fell 7.3%, so prices rose less than profits.

For individual stocks, earnings growth explains only about a fifth of the differences in price returns, and that share barely changes between one and five years (an R-squared of 0.22 to 0.23). Since price is EPS times P/E, the rest comes from changes in each stock's multiple.

Methodology

A stock's price return over any period splits exactly into two parts: the growth in its earnings per share and the change in its price-to-earnings multiple. If EPS grows 10% and the P/E rises 5%, the price rises 1.10 × 1.05 − 1 = 15.5%. Dividends come on top of that for the total return. Each part is annualized as a geometric rate over the period, so the parts multiply back to the annualized return: (1 + total) = (1 + EPS growth) × (1 + P/E change) × (1 + dividends).

EPS is trailing four-quarter reported EPS, the adjusted figure companies and analysts quote, from Alpha Vantage, as it was known on each date: a quarter counts once its report date has passed. Prices are Yahoo Finance closes, adjusted for splits for the P/E and for splits and dividends for the total return. The periods end at the latest close and start 1, 3 and 5 years earlier.

A stock with a loss over the trailing four quarters at either end of the period has no meaningful P/E or EPS growth, so it's left out of the stock-level split and the scatter. The S&P 500 and sector figures add up earnings and market value across all of today's members at today's share counts, so they include those stocks, losses and all, and the largest companies count the most. Because the members are today's, stocks that left the index during the period aren't included.

The fit across stocks is the R-squared of annualized log price return on annualized log EPS growth: the share of the differences in stocks' price returns that lines up with differences in their earnings growth. The rank correlation is shown too, since a few extreme growth rates can sway the R-squared.

Sources: Alpha Vantage (reported EPS and report dates, shares outstanding), Yahoo Finance (prices).
