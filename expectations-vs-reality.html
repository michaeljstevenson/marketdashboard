Reading this page

Each January (sometimes late December of the prior year), major banks and independent strategists publish where they expect the S&P 500 to finish that calendar year. Financial media compile these into a consensus figure — an average or median across roughly 10-20 named strategists — which is what's plotted here as the "target." That figure gets revised throughout the year as conditions change, but the chart uses the original, as-published consensus, since that's the actual forecast being tested against what happened.

The pattern that shows up across almost every year in this dataset: strategists cluster their targets around a "normal" high-single-digit gain, and actual returns are far more volatile than that — both to the upside (2019, 2021, 2023, 2024, all beat consensus by wide margins) and the downside (2018, 2022). The years targets came closest to reality — 2005, 2010, 2016 — were also some of the more unremarkable years in the sample, without a sharp regime change up or down. That's not a coincidence: consensus forecasting is structurally better suited to calm years than volatile ones, because the process anchors on recent trends and rarely assigns much probability to a sharp break from them.

None of this means the forecasts are useless — they're a reasonable read on sell-side sentiment and positioning at a point in time. But treating a January target as a meaningful prediction of where the index will actually be twelve months later hasn't been supported by a quarter century of data. Not once in this entire 2000–2025 span has the Wall Street consensus called for a down year, even though the S&P 500 actually finished lower in 7 of those 26 years.

On sourcing the earlier years: 2000 and 2002–2007 weren't available as press-reported consensus figures the way the other years are — no outlet's archived coverage of those specific years turned up a precise, citable number. Instead, those seven years' forecast-return values were read directly off a Bloomberg-sourced scatter chart published by 6meridian.com (an advisory firm's retrospective, itself crediting Bespoke Investment Group's methodology), which plots each year's strategist-forecast return against its actual return. The implied target price shown in the table for those years is back-calculated from that forecast return and the prior year's close, rather than quoted from an original survey.

On the herd-mentality tiles: individual named-bank targets are far less consistently archived than the aggregate consensus figure — press coverage that lists every named strategist's specific number (rather than just the average) is really only reliable from about 2013 onward, and even within that window, coverage quality varies a lot by year (25 named firms for 2020, as few as 5 for 2014). 2000–2007, 2009, 2011, 2012, and 2015 have no verifiable individual-firm breakdown at all and are marked "no data" rather than estimated. Where a firm revised its target mid-year, only the original December/January call is used.

Sources: BusinessWeek (via Seeking Alpha archive), CNNMoney/Yahoo Finance strategist roundups, Bloomberg surveys (via CNBC, Financial Samurai, globalmarketsinvestor.substack.com compilations, and 6meridian.com's Bloomberg-sourced retrospective chart), Barron's Big Money Poll. Actual year-end closes from public S&P 500 index data.
