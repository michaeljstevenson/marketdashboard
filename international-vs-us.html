As of October 7, 2026

International stocks have led the US since November 2024, but only by 7.8%, and most of that came back out this year. The lead peaked at 23.4% in February 2026, and over the last month developed markets fell 5.0% while SPY rose 0.8%. The developed/US ratio still sits at its lowest level since 2003.

Over the past 12 months, developed markets trailed the US by 4.8 points, and the dollar explains all of it and more. Local stock markets actually beat the US by 2.3 points (the hedged fund minus SPY), but the stronger dollar took 7.2 points off what a US investor earned.

Diversification abroad has been unusually effective. The 36-month correlation between EFA and SPY is 0.63, the lowest reading since 2004, and returns inside the index are far apart: Japan is up 24.8% over the past year and emerging markets 27.0%, while France is down 3.2% and China 20.1%.

Methodology

Unless a section says otherwise, international and US stocks are represented by three ETFs: EFA for developed markets outside the US, EEM for emerging markets, and SPY for the US. Returns include dividends and are in US dollars, so the currency effect a US investor would actually experience is already built in. The ratio series starts at 100 in April 2003, when EEM launched.

Each month is classified by how much EUR/USD changed over the prior 3 months. A rise of more than 2% is "Dollar Weakening," a fall of more than 2% is "Dollar Strengthening," and anything in between is "Flat." EUR/USD is only one currency pair, so this is a rough stand-in for the dollar's direction. The idea being tested is that a weaker dollar should boost international returns for a US investor. Each regime has only about 75 to 115 months, so treat the averages as suggestive.

The scatter shows Pearson (linear) and Spearman (rank) correlation so one or two extreme months can't drive the result. A low r² is expected, since one currency pair rarely explains much of monthly stock returns.

The currency split from 2015 uses HEFA, the currency-hedged version of EFA, which launched in January 2014. HEFA holds EFA and sells the foreign currencies forward each month, so its return is close to what the same stocks earned in their own currencies. The EFA minus SPY gap is split into two parts that add up exactly: HEFA minus SPY (local markets) and EFA minus HEFA (currency). The arithmetic is exact, but HEFA is only an approximation of local-currency returns. A currency hedge earns the gap between US and foreign short-term interest rates. With US short-term rates above euro, yen and Swiss franc rates throughout the period since 2015, HEFA has picked up anywhere from under one to about three percentage points a year from the hedge on top of the local stock return. That carry sits in the local-market part and comes out of the currency part. The two funds' small fee and tracking differences also land in the split.

The Ken French view, which starts in 1975, uses Kenneth French's Major Markets index, which averages his country portfolios using EAFE plus Canada country weights and publishes value-weighted returns both in US dollars and in local currencies. There the currency part is the dollar return minus the local return, and the local-market part is the local return minus the US market return (Ken French's Mkt-RF plus RF). That split is exact for the index, with no hedging carry in it. The index includes Canada, which EFA leaves out, so its yearly returns can differ from EFA's by a few percentage points. Ken French updates this file once a year, and the latest published month is …. The tooltips and the leg table also show the change in the ICE US Dollar Index (DXY), a basket of six currencies weighted mostly to the euro, as a cross-check. DXY is used because its history runs back to 1971, while the Federal Reserve's broad trade-weighted dollar index (DTWEXBGS) starts in 2006.

The leadership-cycle line divides the international cumulative dollar return by the US cumulative dollar return, month by month. International is the Major Markets index through … and EFA after that. The US is Ken French's US market return through … and SPY after that. The last point is the latest close, so the current month is partial. A leg ends when the line reverses at least 20% from its latest peak or trough, measured in log terms so rises and falls are treated alike. A 15% threshold adds a one-month leg in 1990 and splits the 2008 to 2009 swings into short legs. A 30% threshold merges the 1983 to 1988 run with its 1984 pause. The first leg starts where the data starts, so it is cut short. The current leg is still open and only becomes a completed leg once the line reverses 20%. In the leg table, (1 + currency) × (1 + local vs. US) = 1 + relative move. After the Major Markets data ends, the local-currency return comes from HEFA, so a leg running past that point picks up a little hedging carry.

The heat map shows dividend-inclusive total returns in US dollars for single-country iShares ETFs, the same funds the Country / Sector Scorecard uses where the two overlap. All periods end at the latest close. YTD starts from the prior year's last close, and 3Y and 5Y are annualized. "Minus US" subtracts SPY's return for the same period, in percentage points. Shading is scaled within each column, and any cell at or beyond the column's 85th percentile of absolute size gets the full shade. Each country fund holds that market's large and mid caps, so a few large companies can drive it.

The rolling correlation uses complete months only. The ETF lines start 36 months after each fund's first full month, in 2004 for EFA and 2006 for EEM. The Ken French lines take the comparison back to 1977 for developed markets (Major Markets index vs. US market) and 1992 for emerging markets (Ken French's emerging-markets market return, Mkt-RF plus RF, vs. US market). A lower correlation means international stocks moved more independently of US stocks, which is when holding them did more to smooth a US investor's returns. Correlation doesn't show which side returned more.
