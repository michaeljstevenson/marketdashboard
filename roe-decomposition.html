Methodology

The DuPont identity splits return on equity into three multiplicative pieces: ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Leverage, where Net Margin is net income ÷ revenue, Asset Turnover is revenue ÷ total assets, and Leverage (the "equity multiplier") is total assets ÷ total shareholder equity. Multiply the three together and the assets and revenue terms cancel, leaving net income ÷ equity — ROE itself. The point of decomposing it is that two companies can post an identical ROE while getting there in opposite ways: a grocery chain typically earns a high turnover on razor-thin margins, a bank or utility typically earns a modest margin and turnover but layers on far more leverage, and a software company typically earns most of its ROE from margin alone.

Universe is the full S&P 500. For every constituent, Alpha Vantage's INCOME_STATEMENT and BALANCE_SHEET endpoints (quarterly, up to 28 quarters back) supply the trailing four quarters needed for TTM net income and TTM revenue, matched by fiscal quarter-end date against the corresponding balance-sheet report. Margin and Turnover use the TTM (trailing four quarters) flow figures over the latest single quarter's asset base — the same "TTM flow, latest-quarter stock" convention as ROIC vs. Cost of Capital. Leverage and ROE use the latest quarter's equity. Companies with non-positive latest-quarter book shareholder equity are excluded entirely, not shown with a distorted ratio — the same real limitation flagged on the ROIC/WACC page: aggressive, sustained buyback programs (McDonald's, Starbucks, and a number of other well-known names) can drive book equity negative, which breaks both the leverage and ROE math here. A company is also excluded if fewer than four consecutive matched quarters are available, or if TTM revenue or the latest quarter's total assets aren't positive.

Dominant driver. Each company's margin, turnover, and leverage are each divided by that component's own market-wide median (an index where 100 = market-typical), rather than a z-score — the three components live on very different scales (margin in percent, turnover and leverage as small multiples), so a raw z-score would let whichever has the smallest cross-sectional spread dominate by arithmetic accident rather than economic meaning. The component with the highest index value is tagged as that company's dominant driver. "Quality ROE leaders" filters to companies at or below market-median leverage (leverage index ≤ 100) before ranking by ROE — a screen for ROE earned through margin and efficiency rather than balance-sheet leverage.

The trend charts and structural-break test. Aggregate quarterly figures (sum of net income, revenue, assets, and equity across every constituent with data that quarter, not an average of per-company ratios) build a market-wide quarterly series back roughly seven years, trimmed to the longest run of consecutive calendar quarters. Turnover and ROE on this series are annualized (×4) from a single quarter's flow rather than a rolling TTM, so they're a noisier, single-quarter read — useful for a multi-year trend, not meant to match the TTM cross-sectional snapshot used everywhere else on the page. Each series is regressed against a plain quarter index, split into two equal-length halves (oldest vs. most recent, the same equal-sample-size convention Factor Analysis uses for its own subsample checks), and a Chow test asks whether the trend's slope genuinely differs between the two halves — the same test AI Capex Tracker runs on capex intensity.

The cross-sectional tests (leverage vs. ROE scatter, and the margin/turnover/leverage factor table) are single-snapshot regressions using the same Pearson-and-Spearman two-method check as Factor Analysis — they describe the current cross-section, not a forward-return prediction.
