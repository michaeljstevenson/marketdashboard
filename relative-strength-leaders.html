Methodology

Each stock's return is measured over the last 21 trading days (1 month) and 63 trading days (3 months), including dividends. Relative return is (1 + stock return) ÷ (1 + S&P 500 return) − 1 rather than a simple subtraction, so it stays accurate for larger moves.

A stock is a "Leader" if its 3-month relative return is in the top fifth of the index, a "Laggard" if it's in the bottom fifth, and "Neutral" otherwise. Rankings use 3 months rather than 1 month, since 1-month returns are noisier and more easily swung by a single event (an earnings beat, a guidance cut).

The persistence test asks whether this week's rank says anything about what happens next. Comparing one week's 3-month rank with the next week's would be misleading, since the two windows share almost all of the same trading days and would line up almost automatically. Instead, each stock's rank at one point is paired with its return over the following period, which uses entirely new trading days. A positive, significant relationship would mean leaders tend to keep outperforming. A flat or negative one would mean relative strength fades or says nothing about the future at this horizon. Both Pearson (linear) and Spearman (rank) fits are shown.
