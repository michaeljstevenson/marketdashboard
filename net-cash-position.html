Methodology

Net cash ratio. For every S&P 500 constituent, Alpha Vantage's BALANCE_SHEET endpoint (latest quarter) supplies cash & short-term investments and total debt. Net Cash = cash − total debt (the same field-selection logic Margin & Leverage Cycle and ROIC vs. Cost of Capital already use: Alpha Vantage's combined shortLongTermDebtTotal field where available, falling back to summing short- and long-term debt separately). Net Cash Ratio = Net Cash ÷ market capitalization × 100, so a company with a $50B cash cushion and a $40B one are treated very differently depending on how big the rest of the company is — a raw dollar figure alone would just be a market-cap ranking in disguise.

Why Financials, Real Estate, and Utilities are excluded. Unlike Cash Conversion Cycle's exclusion of those same three sectors (which happens naturally, because banks/REITs/utilities mostly don't report inventory or cost-of-revenue at all), this page's exclusion is a deliberate choice: those sectors do report cash and debt figures, but the figures don't mean the same thing. A bank's balance sheet is funded mostly by customer deposits, not the kind of "debt" a net-cash-vs-market-cap ratio is trying to capture; a REIT's or utility's debt is structural, regulated leverage against real assets, not a discretionary financing choice comparable to an industrial or tech company's. Including them would mix three very different kinds of "leverage" onto one scale and make the sector chart misleading rather than informative.

The value of cash. A large cash cushion is genuinely ambiguous in corporate-finance theory: it can be a safety margin and a source of strategic optionality (dry powder for acquisitions, R&D, or downturns), or it can be read as capital the company isn't putting to productive use — the "agency cost of free cash flow" concern, and the broader question Faulkender & Wang (2006, Journal of Finance) and the corporate-cash-holdings literature that followed ask directly: what is a marginal dollar of cash actually worth to shareholders? This page runs two single-snapshot cross-sectional tests of that question (Pearson and Spearman, the same two-method check as Factor Analysis): net cash ratio against each company's trailing 3-month return relative to SPY (reused from Relative Strength Leaders/Laggards' own weekly snapshot rather than a second price sweep), and net cash ratio against ROIC vs. Cost of Capital's own return-on-invested-capital-minus-cost-of-capital spread (reused from that page's own published snapshot) — does a bigger cash pile coincide with the company actually earning more above its cost of capital, or is idle cash a drag on measured returns instead? Both are contemporaneous associations, not forward-return predictions: they ask what's true right now, not what a large cash position today implies about returns next quarter.

A company is included only when both a usable debt figure and a usable cash figure are present on the latest balance sheet and it has a positive market cap on record — a missing field is excluded, never assumed to be zero. Sector, company name, and market cap come from Sector Beeswarm. One-time snapshot, no recurring schedule — matches this site's convention for full-universe fundamentals sweeps added since 2026-09-16; quarterly balance-sheet figures don't move day to day.
