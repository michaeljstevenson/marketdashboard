How to read this

The implied equity risk premium answers a different question than a historical-average ERP does: instead of asking "what did stocks beat bonds by in the past," it asks "given where the S&P 500 is priced today, and what cash flows (dividends and buybacks) investors expect going forward, what rate of return is priced in — and how much of that is compensation for risk above the risk-free rate?" It's the equity-market equivalent of a bond's yield to maturity: an internal rate of return solved for from the current price and expected cash flows, not a backward-looking average.

A rising implied ERP means investors are demanding more compensation to hold stocks relative to bonds — often a sign of rising risk aversion or falling confidence, even if the index price itself hasn't moved much (a higher premium can be priced in through slower expected cash flow growth). A falling ERP means the market is pricing in more optimism, or complacency, per dollar of risk. The implied expected equity return (ERP + the risk-free rate) is the total forward-looking return the market is pricing into stocks today — useful as a sanity check against portfolio return assumptions (like the ones in the Portfolio Simulator).

Limitations: this is a model-based estimate, not an observed market price — it depends on assumptions about future cash flow growth and payout (dividends + buybacks) that Damodaran documents but that reasonable analysts could set differently. It's also annual data, updated once a year each January, not a live daily series.

Source: Aswath Damodaran, NYU Stern — Implied Equity Risk Premiums (FCFE basis)
