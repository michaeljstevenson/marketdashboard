Is the implied ERP a statistically significant predictor?

These two scatter plots test two different questions, and the distinction matters. The left plot asks whether a given year's implied ERP relates to that same year's realized return — but that relationship is mostly mechanical, not predictive: the implied ERP for a year is calculated from that year's own year-end price, so a year that ends with a falling market mechanically produces a higher year-end ERP for the same year (lower price, same expected cash flows, means a higher discount rate solves the valuation equation). A negative same-year correlation is close to what the math guarantees, not evidence the model forecasts anything.

The right plot is the real test: does a given year's implied ERP predict the following year's return, the way the risk-compensation theory behind it would imply (a higher premium should mean investors are being paid more to hold stocks, and should on average realize a higher subsequent return)? Here the relationship runs in the theoretically correct direction — higher ERP is associated with a higher next-year return — and lands right at the conventional 5% significance threshold. But the R² is only about 6%: even taking the relationship at face value, the implied ERP explains a small fraction of next year's return, with most of the year-to-year variation coming from something else entirely.

Caveats on the significance itself: this is 63-64 annual observations — a small sample by statistical standards, which limits how much confidence any single p-value deserves. Three related relationships were tested on the same dataset (same-year, predictive, and implied-expected-return-vs-next-year in the earlier chat analysis); testing multiple hypotheses on one dataset raises the odds that at least one clears a 5% threshold by chance, an effect these p-values don't correct for. The p-values themselves come from a standard t-test assuming independent errors year to year — annual valuation-based series like this one often show some autocorrelation (a high-ERP year tends to be followed by other high-ERP years), which if present would overstate significance. None of this means the relationship is fake; it means "right at p≈0.05 with R²≈6%" is a genuinely weak, borderline result, not a strong or conclusive one — consistent with a broad academic literature finding that valuation-based predictors (dividend yield, CAPE, implied ERP) have some real but modest power to forecast long-run returns, and very little power over any single year.

How to read this

The implied equity risk premium answers a different question than a historical-average ERP does: instead of asking "what did stocks beat bonds by in the past," it asks "given where the S&P 500 is priced today, and what cash flows (dividends and buybacks) investors expect going forward, what rate of return is priced in — and how much of that is compensation for risk above the risk-free rate?" It's the equity-market equivalent of a bond's yield to maturity: an internal rate of return solved for from the current price and expected cash flows, not a backward-looking average.

A rising implied ERP means investors are demanding more compensation to hold stocks relative to bonds — often a sign of rising risk aversion or falling confidence, even if the index price itself hasn't moved much (a higher premium can be priced in through slower expected cash flow growth). A falling ERP means the market is pricing in more optimism, or complacency, per dollar of risk. The implied expected equity return (ERP + the risk-free rate) is the total forward-looking return the market is pricing into stocks today — useful as a sanity check against portfolio return assumptions (like the ones in the Portfolio Simulator).

The bars add what actually happened: the S&P 500's realized return for that same year, so you can see directly how often — and by how much — the market's forward-looking premium missed the outcome. The gap runs both ways: 2008's implied ERP rose to a then-record 6.43% heading into a year the index fell 38.5%, while 2013's modest 4.96% implied ERP preceded a 29.6% realized gain. The implied ERP isn't a forecast of that year's return — it's priced off long-run expected cash flows and typically resolves over many years, not one — so a wide single-year gap isn't itself evidence the model is wrong, but the persistent gap between the smooth premium and the noisy outcome is the point: realized returns are dominated by short-run sentiment and macro shocks the model doesn't try to capture.

Limitations: this is a model-based estimate, not an observed market price — it depends on assumptions about future cash flow growth and payout (dividends + buybacks) that Damodaran documents but that reasonable analysts could set differently. It's also annual data, updated once a year each January, not a live daily series. The realized-return bars are price return only (year-end index level to year-end index level) — they exclude the S&P 500's dividend yield (historically ~1.5-2%/year), so total return in any given year was somewhat higher than the bar shown.
