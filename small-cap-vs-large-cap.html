Methodology

Small-caps and large-caps are represented by two ETFs: IWM (Russell 2000) for small-caps and SPY (S&P 500) for large-caps, with MDY (S&P MidCap 400) added as a mid-cap step in the return table. Returns include dividends. The ratio starts at 100 in May 2000, when IWM launched.

Each month is classified by the change in the Fed funds rate over the prior 3 months. Up more than 0.10 percentage points is "Hiking," down more than 0.10 is "Cutting," and anything in between is "Holding." The idea being tested is that small-caps, which tend to carry more floating-rate debt and have less pricing power, should be more sensitive to rate changes. Each regime only has a few dozen months, so treat the averages as suggestive.

The scatter shows Pearson (linear) and Spearman (rank) correlation so one or two extreme months can't drive the result. A low r² is expected, since one macro variable rarely explains much of monthly returns.

Prices are Yahoo Finance adjusted closes, which add back dividends and splits. The Fed funds rate (FRED series FEDFUNDS) and the 10-year Treasury yield (FRED series GS10) are monthly averages from the St. Louis Fed. The 10-year change in the scatter is the change in that monthly average.

The ratio drawdown divides the IWM/SPY ratio by its highest earlier value and subtracts one. It shows how much relative ground small caps have given up since their best point against large caps.

The S&P SmallCap 600 only adds a company when its latest quarter and the sum of its last four quarters both show positive earnings. The Russell 2000 holds the 2,000 U.S. companies ranked after the largest 1,000 by market value, with no profit test. The earnings screen applies when a company is added, so some members still post losses, but it tilts the S&P 600 toward profitable companies. IJR and IWM stand in for the two indexes. Both are total return from adjusted closes, with fund fees already in the prices. The calendar-year count leaves out the partial first and current years.

The long history uses Ken French's Portfolios Formed on Size. Each June, every NYSE, AMEX and NASDAQ stock in CRSP is sorted into fifths by market value using NYSE breakpoints, and the file reports value-weighted monthly returns from July 1926. Small is the bottom fifth and large the top fifth. Because the breakpoints come from NYSE stocks, the bottom fifth holds several times as many companies as the top fifth and reaches into micro caps smaller than most Russell 2000 members. Relative returns compound small divided by large, the same way as the ETF ratio. The file is updated about once a month and runs one to two months behind.

For the extreme-spread study, each month gets its trailing 3-year and 5-year annualized small vs. large return from the Ken French quintiles. Months in the bottom or top tenth of all those readings are compared with the annualized relative return over the next 1, 3 and 5 years. The tenths are set using the whole history, which no one could have known at the time. Forward windows overlap from one month to the next, so the t-statistics use Newey-West standard errors, and the table counts non-overlapping windows and separate episodes (runs of extreme months less than a year apart). Each extreme has only five or six episodes, and a few periods such as the early 1930s, the mid-1970s and the late 1990s carry most of the result, so the evidence is thin even where a t-statistic looks large.

Today's reading uses Ken French returns through the latest month published and the IWM/SPY ratio for the months since, with the current month counted to date. IWM and SPY follow the quintile spread closely without matching it (monthly correlation … since 2000), so the percentile is approximate.
