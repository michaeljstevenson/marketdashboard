Methodology

Universe is the full S&P 500. For every constituent, Yahoo Finance's split events return its entire historical split record; events from the last 15 years feed the frequency chart above, at zero extra cost since it's one call per ticker regardless of history depth. Only events within the last 5 years get a full price-history pull (daily adjusted closes, full history) for the event study and tables below — a heavier per-ticker payload than the split events, and older splits aren't what a reader wants a price chart built around anyway.

A real data-quality issue surfaced while building this page: Alpha Vantage's SPLITS endpoint mixes in non-split corporate-action adjustment factors. GE, for example, shows "splits" of 1.2530 (2024-04-02) and 1.2810 (2023-01-04) — these are the GE Vernova and GE HealthCare spin-off share-adjustment ratios, not stock splits GE ever announced (its real splits are the clean 3-for-1 in 2000 and 1-for-8 reverse split in 2021, both correctly kept). Every event is checked against an allowlist of the ratios real splits actually use (2-for-1, 3-for-1, 3-for-2, 10-for-1, 1-for-8, etc.) within a tight 0.15% tolerance; anything that doesn't match — a spin-off adjustment, most likely — is dropped rather than miscounted as a split.

The event study normalizes each qualifying forward split's own adjusted-close price series to 0% on the last trading day before its effective date, does the same for SPY over the identical calendar dates, and plots the average difference (stock return minus SPY return) at each trading-day offset from 20 days before to 120 days after. This is a simple relative-return comparison, not a CAPM-style beta-adjusted abnormal return — a stock's normal volatility and market sensitivity aren't backed out, so the same caveat that applies to every other relative-return chart on this site applies here too. Reverse splits are tracked and shown in the tables (rare among current S&P 500 names — most companies that need one no longer qualify for the index) but excluded from the averaged path and the split-size regression, since a reverse split's usual context — a low-priced, often distressed stock avoiding delisting — is a different signal entirely from a forward split's "the stock did well enough that management wants a lower nominal price."

The split-size regression and the post-split return's one-sample significance both use the same Pearson/Spearman-style linear-regression machinery as Factor Analysis and this site's other regression checks, including its normal-approximation p-value rather than a small-sample t-distribution — consistent with, not more rigorous than, the rest of the site's methodology.
