As of the October 6, 2026 close

Sectors are moving more independently than at almost any point since 1999. The average correlation among the nine original sector ETFs over the last 63 trading days is 0.13, the lowest 1% of daily readings in that history, and the first factor explains just 34.5% of their combined movement, also a 1st-percentile reading.

The sectors are still moving a lot. Dispersion, how far apart their one-month returns are, sits in the 73rd percentile, so they're moving sharply in different directions. That's when sector weights matter most for a portfolio.

Almost every pair is less correlated than a year ago. Energy has come loose the most, with its correlation to Industrials down 0.45 and to Consumer Discretionary down 0.42, and Technology's correlation to Communication Services is down 0.40.

Methodology

Daily returns for the 11 sector ETFs and SPY over roughly the last 2 years. Every pair of sectors gets a Pearson correlation of their daily returns.

The rolling correlation uses a 63-trading-day (about one quarter) window. For each day, it averages all 55 sector-pair correlations over the prior 63 days, then moves forward one day. The scatter plots that series against the S&P 500's realized volatility over the same 63-day window (standard deviation of daily returns × √252), with Pearson and Spearman regression. Back-to-back days in a rolling window share almost all their data, so the number of truly independent observations is much smaller than it looks. Treat the r and p-values as a rough read on direction and strength.

The heatmap leaves out SPY, since it's an average of the sectors rather than a peer. Each sector's correlation to SPY is shown in its own table below the heatmap.
