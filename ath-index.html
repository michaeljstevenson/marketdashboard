Why two data sources

This page used to compute "at an all-time high" from Alpha Vantage's daily price history — but that history turned out to be capped at roughly 26 years for every symbol tested, starting around 1999–2000 regardless of how long the company's actually been listed. For long-established names (3M, Amgen, Eaton, and others), that understates the true all-time high: 3M's real peak (reached before 1999) sits well above its current price, but Alpha Vantage's truncated history couldn't see that and flagged it as a new high anyway.

The current count and list above now come from TradingView's own all-time-high figure instead, which is built from deeper history. The long-run chart below still uses the Alpha Vantage series, since it has years of daily history to draw a trend from and the general shape is still informative — but treat its day-to-day count as an upper-bound estimate, not a precise figure, for the reason above.

A name counts as being at an all-time high when its intraday high reaches its recorded all-time high for the day. A high reading means strength is broad — many names participating, not just a handful of mega-caps carrying the market. A reading near zero doesn't necessarily mean a bear market; it can simply mean the market has pulled back from a recent peak across the board, which is normal even in an uptrend.

How this is computed

Mirrors the all-time-high methodology on the other tab: a name counts as being at an all-time low when its intraday low reaches its recorded all-time low for the day, sourced from TradingView's screener (Low.All field). There's no Alpha Vantage-based fallback or long-run chart here, since the flawed Alpha Vantage history that motivated the ATH fix never had a comparable all-time-low computation to begin with — this tab is TradingView-only.

Source: TradingView Screener

Reading this chart

The bars show the count of S&P 500 constituents at a fresh all-time high minus the count at a fresh all-time low, each trading day — a classic breadth measure. Positive and rising means strength is broadening; negative means more names are breaking down than breaking out, which can show up before the index itself rolls over. The line overlays the S&P 500 level itself for context.

The historical bars come from the same Alpha Vantage daily history that backs the All-Time Highs tab's long-run chart, so they carry the same caveat: that history is capped at roughly 26 years per symbol, understating the true all-time high or low for long-listed names and skewing older counts on both sides. Today's bar is overridden with the accurate TradingView-based count once it's populated for the day.

Sources: Alpha Vantage (historical bars & S&P 500 level) · TradingView Screener (today's bar)
