Commentary

As of the week ending October 2, 2026

Index options are showing little fear. SPY's put/call ratio was 1.21 for the week ending October 2, the 18th percentile of weekly readings since 2008, and QQQ's 1.06 was in the 16th. Against their own past year both are ordinary, near the 49th and 35th percentiles, so the low readings reflect a long drift down in how many puts trade relative to calls.

A high put/call ratio has a reputation as a contrarian buy signal, and since 2009 that has held only for small caps. When IWM's ratio was in the top fifth of its past year, IWM returned 4.98% over the next three months on average, against 1.96% from the bottom fifth (t = 3.0). For SPY and QQQ the differences are small and not significant, and SPY's even leans the other way.

Among individual stocks, the median S&P 500 member's ratio is 0.59, and only a quarter of members have more put volume than call volume. Financials are the most put-heavy sector (0.87), and a stock's ratio still has no measurable link to how it did over the past three months.

Methodology

The put/call ratio is the volume of put options traded divided by the volume of call options traded, across all of a stock's options on the latest trading day. Above 1.0 means more puts traded than calls. Below 1.0 means more calls than puts.

Put/call ratios are skewed. A few thinly traded stocks can print a ratio of 3 or 4 on a single low-volume day, while most liquid stocks sit in a narrow range. The market and sector figures use the median for that reason, with the mean shown alongside so the gap is visible.

The SPY, QQQ and IWM history is the full-chain ratio on the last trading day of every week since January 2008, from Alpha Vantage's historical put/call data. The near- and far-dated lines take the median of the ratios Alpha Vantage reports for each expiration, which is a simpler measure than a volume-weighted ratio because each expiration counts once however much it traded.

The forward test ranks each week's ratio against the same fund's previous 52 weeks, so a slow change in the ratio's normal level over the years doesn't decide the result. Returns are the fund's total return starting at the next close after the ratio is known. Weekly windows overlap, so the slope of return on the percentile is tested with a Newey-West standard error.

The momentum test uses Pearson and Spearman correlation between each stock's put/call ratio and its 3-month return relative to the S&P 500. A positive, significant relationship would mean bearish options positioning is concentrated in stocks that have already lagged, which fits put buying as insurance on recent losers rather than a forward-looking signal. A weak or flat relationship would mean positioning isn't just following where the stock has already been.

A single day's put/call ratio is noisy and reflects hedging as much as conviction. A high ratio ahead of a big event (earnings, an FDA decision) can just mean someone is protecting a bullish position. The forward test above shows how much of a signal the fund-level ratio has carried since 2009.
