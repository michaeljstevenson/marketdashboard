Post-Earnings Announcement Drift

Does a stock keep drifting in the direction of its earnings surprise for weeks afterward, as the academic literature says it should? This tests it on about ten years of S&P 500 earnings reports: the price reaction around each report and the drift over the following 60 trading days relative to SPY, by size of surprise.

Methodology

Post-Earnings Announcement Drift (PEAD) is one of the oldest anomalies in finance, first documented by Ball and Brown in 1968. After a company beats or misses the consensus estimate, its stock tends to keep moving in that direction, relative to the market, for weeks afterward. That's surprising because the result is public the moment it's reported, so an efficient market should price it in right away.

The surprise is (actual EPS − consensus estimate) ÷ |consensus estimate|, for each reported quarter, using Alpha Vantage's reported and estimated EPS. Quarters where the estimate was within 5 cents of zero are left out, since a tiny estimate turns small misses into huge percentages.

The data doesn't say whether a company reported before the open or after the close, so day 0 is the first trading day on or after the report date and the reaction runs from the close of day -1 to the close of day +1, which covers either case. Drift is measured from the close of day +1, so it starts only after the news is fully out. All returns are relative to SPY: (1 + stock return) ÷ (1 + SPY return) − 1.

Within each calendar quarter of report dates, reports are ranked by surprise and split into fifths, so a quarter in which nearly every company beat doesn't fill the top group by itself. The test is the gap between the top and bottom fifths, computed quarter by quarter and then averaged, with a Newey-West standard error because neighboring quarters' 60-day windows overlap. A t-statistic above about 2 in absolute value is unlikely to be chance.

The ten years cover today's S&P 500 members only. Companies that left the index, including some that fell out after large misses, aren't included, which probably makes the bottom fifth look better than it was. The latest-reports tables show each company's most recent report from the last 100 trading days, with drift from the day after the report to the latest close.
