As of the October 6, 2026 close, 1,932 Russell 2000 members

The typical Russell 2000 stock trades about $12 million a day, against about $344 million for the typical S&P 500 member, and by the Amihud measure it moves about 40 times more for each dollar traded. Its estimated trading cost, from the Corwin-Schultz high-low spread, is 1.06% against 0.65%.

That gap has narrowed over the past year. Median daily dollar volume for Russell 2000 members rose from $8.9 million in September 2025 to $12.6 million in September 2026, and the illiquidity gap with the S&P 500 fell from about 64 times to about 42 times.

Within the index, cost falls with size. The smallest fifth of members (under about $350 million in market value) has an estimated spread of 1.36%, the largest fifth 0.93%. Whether the gap widens in weeks when small caps lag couldn't be detected over this past year (r = 0.10, t = 0.67).

All figures cover the latest 63 trading days. $ Volume and Turnover are medians of daily values, and Turnover is shares traded as a percentage of shares outstanding. Amihud ratio is illiquidity (price impact) per $1M of dollar volume traded, so higher means less liquid. C-S Spread is the Corwin-Schultz estimated bid-ask spread, as a percentage of price. Zero-vol is the share of sessions with no shares traded. Fifth 1 is the smallest. Sectors are TradingView's.

Methodology

"Small-Cap" here means the members of the Russell 2000, taken each week from TradingView's screener because the fund and index providers' holdings files refuse scripted downloads. Members not listed on NYSE, Nasdaq or NYSE American, and members with no Yahoo Finance price in the last five sessions, are left out. "Large-Cap" means the members of the S&P 500. Size fifths rank the Russell 2000 by current market value, and sectors are TradingView's own categories, which differ from GICS.

Dollar volume = closing price × shares traded. Turnover = shares traded ÷ shares outstanding, using TradingView's current share count. The Amihud (2002) illiquidity ratio = |daily return| ÷ dollar volume, scaled to price impact per $1M traded, so a stock that moves a lot on little trading scores high. The Corwin-Schultz (2012) estimator infers the bid-ask spread from two consecutive days' high and low prices, after shifting the second day's range by any overnight gap as the paper does. It can come out negative in calm periods, which is a known quirk, and those values are set to zero. For heavily traded stocks the estimate runs well above the quoted spread, since part of each day's range is ordinary price movement, so its figures are best compared with each other.

Each stock's measures cover its latest 63 trading days (about three months), or 21 (about one month) on the cards, since daily dollar volume and the Amihud ratio are very noisy (one large block trade can move them by a factor of ten). Each group's figure is the median across its members, and the monthly charts cover roughly the last 13 months. Membership is today's list, so earlier months include companies that joined the index later and leave out ones that have since left it.

The regression tests whether liquidity gets worse in weeks when small-caps underperform. Each point is one week, since a year of daily data holds only 12 months. X is IWM's return relative to SPY that week. Y is the percent change in the Russell 2000 / S&P 500 Amihud ratio that week. A negative correlation means liquidity worsens when small-caps lag: weak small-cap weeks pair with a widening liquidity gap. A positive one means the opposite. Pearson (linear) and Spearman (rank) are shown side by side, with Newey-West t-statistics (4 lags). A low r² just means one explains little of the other.
